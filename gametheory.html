3  Conflict resolution in ecology and evolution: war and peace

🚧 This chapter is under construction. Content may change.

In the previous chapter we looked at the decisions of individual animals assuming that the the decisions where taken by each forager alone. For instance, for the sit-and-wait predator we assumed that the territory could be as large as the predator wanted, and that the optimal territory size was the one where the energy per unit time was maximized. But what happens if the territory is so large that it starts intruding in the territory of the neighbors? Or in other words, what happens if the optimal territory size is for instance 1 \(m^2\) , but the density of the predators is so high that there is more than 1 predator per square meter? Then there is a potential conflict between individuals and it may not be possible to have territory sizes that are optimal. More generally, one can say that the decision of an individual now affects the decisions of its conspecifics.

In evolutionary contexts, where decisions or phenotypes are determined by genes, we enter the so called realm of frequency-dependent selection. In contrasts with frequency independent selection, where the fitness of a genotype depends only on the environment, in frequency-dependent selection the fitness of a genotype depends also on the frequency of the different genotypes in the population.

The analysis of this kind of behavior conflict or evolutionary dynamics is the topic of game theory, particularly non-cooperative game theory. Some of the key concepts of game theory for animal behavior were developed by the biologist John Maynard Smith in the 1970’s and beautiful explained in his book “Evolution and the Theory of Games” Maynard Smith (1982). We now know that similar ideas were developed independently by the mathematician John Nash, twenty years earlier in the 1950’s, ideas that would lead to Nash being awarded the Nobel Prize of Economics in 1994.

In this chapter we examine three non-cooperative games that model situations of behavior or evolutionary conflict: the prisoner’s dilemma, the Hawk-Dove game, and the war of attrition. They are called non-cooperative because each “player” does not know what the other “player” is going to do in the game, and the players cannot discuss the strategy that they are going to take in any cooperative game. They are also sometimes described as closed envelope games. This is, each player should write what “strategy” it adopts in each play without knowing the choice of the other player.

3.1 The prisoner’s dillema and the evolution of cooperation

A lot of the empahsis on evolutionary biology and ecology is place on competition, on the idea of “survival of the fittest” and that selection operates at the level of individuals favoring selfish behaviors. However, cooperation is super common in nature, from praire dogs taking turn as sentinels, informing the group of the arrival of a predator, to back scratching in primates. One beautiful explanation for such cooperation is based on the principles of kin selection, developed by Bill Hamilton, or the idea that it makes evolutionary sense to help others if they are genetically related to you, as this increases the probability of the same genes you have to be passed into the next generation. Hamilton’s contribution to this problem was seminal because he noted that genetic relatedness can be particularly high between females in haploid-diploid systems such as bees and ants, leading to a widespread of cooperating in rearing offspring and even the abdication of many individuals from reproducing in favor of assisting the queen. But can cooperation also evolve in the absence of genetic relatedness? This is the problem that can be studied with the prisoner’s dilemma.

In the prisoner’s dilemma game there are two players. it is inspired by a situation where two suspects of being accomplices in a crime are arrested and are being questioned in separate rooms without contact between them. They are faced with the dilemma of cooperating with each other by claiming inocence, or cutting a deal with the investigators by recognizing the responsibility for the crime and by denouncing the other player. If both cooperate they can potentially get both away, but if one accomplice cooperates and the other defects, then the one that cooperated gets the maximum penalty.

We can formalize the prisoner’s dilemma game by stating that each player can choose one of two strategies in each round of the game, defect or cooperate. The pay-off matrix of the Prisoner’s Dilemma, from the pespective of player 1 (rows), playing against player 2 (columns) is

\[ \begin{array}{c|cc}\text{Player 1} \backslash \text{Player 2} & C & D \\ \hline C & 3 & 0 \\D & 5 & 1\end{array} \]

You can think of the pay-off points as a fitness gain in relation to the fitness before the play. The exact payoffs are not so important, and there are other variants of the game with different pay-offs. Instead, it is the relationship between the different pay-offs that determines the best strategy for the game. If the players knew what the other player would do, the best collective strategy would be to cooperate, as they both would receive 3 points for a total of 6 points. If one of the players defects while the other cooperate they accrue collectively only 5 points, while if both defect they would accrue together 2 points. But they don’t know what the other player is going to do (non-cooperative game) and need to make a decision that maximizes their individual fitness. What should they do?

The general solution that both Maynard Smith and Nash came up for these games is that one needs to identify which strategy is the best response to itself. This is called an Evolutionary Stable Strategy (ESS, also known as Nash Equilibrium1) because it is the strategy that when dominant in the population cannot be invaded by any other strategy. Mathematically speaking, the ESS is the strategy \(S^*\) that has the higher payoff when played against itself,

\[ \forall S_i ,\quad E(S^*,S^*) \geq E(S_i,S^*) \tag{3.1}\]

So let’s examine what is the best strategy to itself for each of the two strategies. We need to look at each column of the pay-off matrix. If player 2 plays C, the best response from player 1 is D, as then player 1 receives 5 points instead of 3 if it plays also C. So cooperate is not an EES. What about defect? Well if player 2 plays D, the best response is for player 1 to also play D as it gets 1 point, while if it plays C it gets zero points. This means that defect is an ESS as it is the best response to itself.

This result is somehow paradoxical: how can cooperation be so ubiquitous in nature if the ESS of the prisioner’s dilemma is to defect? Well, the key is that most of these interactions between individuals are not one-off interactions. Instead, cooperation occurs often when there is repeated interactions between individuals. In this case the game takes another form and it is called the iterated prisionner’s dilemma (IPD). For the iterated prisioner’s dilemma, a second strategy emerges as an ESS, and that is the Tit-for-Tat strategy. In the Tit-for-Tat strategy, an individual starts by cooperating in the first interaction. In the following interactions, the individual reciprocates what is interaction partner has done in the last interaction. If the partner (or opponent, depending on the perspective) had played D, the individual plays D in the next interaction. If the partner had played C, then the individual plays C in the next interaction. This means that cooperation can evolve as an overall strategy as long as there are repeated interactions with other individuals and one remembers the results of the last interactions.

I find this a particularly beautiful result. Cooperation can also evolve in nature under kin selection, between closely related individuals. Bill Hamilton was the first biologist to point out that the high relatedness between worker sisters in eusocial insects like ants and bees, which is due to their haplodiploid genetic systems, promotes kin selection (Hamilton 1964). Almost two decades later, in a competition thrown by political scientist Robert Axelrod (Axelrod 2006), where participants where invited to submit computer programs with strategies to play the iterated prisoner’s dilemma, the winning strategy was Tit-for-Tat. It showed that cooperation can also evolve even in the absence of relatedness. The winning strategy was submitted by Anatol Rapoport, a mathematical psychologist.

One way of thinking about evolutionary games is considering a population of haploid individuals with different alleles. The pay-off of playing the game is then the fitness of each individual which can be converted in offspring, or in the case of the iterated prisoner’s dilemma, the fitness becomes the average pay-off of playing multiple rounds of the game with the same opponent. Let’s imagine a population of individuals with the “D” allele meaning that they are defectors. A mutation happens, and the mutant is a tit-for-tater “T”. Can the tit-for-tater invade the population by reproducing faster than the other individuals? Well, the T individual will be loosing all the matches (i.e. sequences of games) against its D partners as it will receive a lower fitness than its D partners who benefit from the first play of T which is to cooperate. So it is not likely to invade, unless there is a strong spatial structure in the game and it can get a couple of neighbors also playing T. If a strong spatial structure exists, a group of T neighbors playing among themselves will be receiving average pay-offs of 3. In contrast, D individuals playing against themselves with an average pay-off of 1, or D individuals playing against T individuals with an average pay-off tending to 0 (for the first game the pay-off for D is 5, but that tends rapidly to zero with more games being played as the tit-for-taters reciprocate D). But the criteria for an ESS is not to be able to invade when rare. It’s to resist invasion when it’s dominant. And if the population is dominated by tit-for-taters, than a mutant playing “D” cannot invade. This is because the individuals playing T against D, receive a lower pay-off than the individuals playing T against T.

3.2 The Hawk-Dove game and animal contests

While in some situations animals cooperate, in many other situations animals fight. Individuals can fight to compete for territory, food, mates and other resources. Humans, unfortunately, also fight often, with many fights escalating to wars. So much of game theory was developed to think about whether to escalate a fight or retreat. The Hawk-Dove game was designed to study this problem. Consider two individuals fighting for a resource with value \(V\). They can play the strategy “Hawk” and escalate the fight or “Dove” and retreat. If individual 1 plays Hawk and individual 2 plays Dove, the individual 1 gets the resource for itself obtaining a pay-off V. If individual 2 responds also with Hawk, then the fight escalates, and assuming symmetric competitors (i.e. equivalent fighting abilities between the two individuals), then half of the time individual 1 wins receiving the resource while half of the time it loses paying the cost of being injured in the fight. If individual 1 plays Dove and individual 2 plays Hawk, individual 1 gets away without anything but also does not pay a cost. Finally, if both individuals play Dove, they share the resource equally, each receiving \(V/2\). Like in the prisoner’s dilemma, this game is a symetric game, so the payoffs of player 2 are the same as the payoffs of player 1 when they reverse roles (i.e. the payoff matrix is symmetric around the diagonal)

The pay-off matrix of the Hawk-Dove game from the perspective of player 1 (rows), playing against player 2 (columns) is

\[ \begin{array}{c|cc}\text{Player 1} \backslash \text{Player 2} & H & D \\ \hline H & V/2-C/2 & V \\D & 0 & V/2\end{array} \]

What is the ESS of the Hawk-Dove game? Let’s examine each of the strategies in turn. For Hawk to be an ESS it has to be the best response to itself. However, if player 2 plays H then whether the best response is D or H depends on the value of the resource in relation to the cost of an injury in the fight. If the value of the resource is greater than the cost of injury, \(V>C\), then the best response is to escalate and play H. Otherwise it is better to play D and retreat. Similarly, for Dove to be an ESS it has to be the best response to itself, but the best response to D is to play H, so D is never an ESS.

In the case that the cost of injury is greater than the value of the resource, \(C>V\), there is no simple strategy that is an ESS. So what should an individual do? The trick is to behave a bit unpredictably. This is, sometimes play dove, sometimes play hawk. Pretty interesting, or? The ESS for an animal contest is to behave unpredictable so that our opponent never knows if you are going to escalate or not. But what should be the proportion of time that one should play Hawk? Well, that comes again for a formal game theoretical analysis. It can be shown (Maynard Smith 1974) that in a game with two pure strategies (e.g Hawk and Dove), a mixed strategy \(M\) is an ESS if,

\[ E(H,M)=E(D,M)=E(M,M) \tag{3.2}\]

Let’s assume that in the mixed strategy one plays Hawk with probability \(p\) and Dove with probability \(1-p\). Then the payoff of a Hawk playing against an individual with the mixed strategy, \(E(H,M)\), is the payoff of playing against a Hawk with probability \(p\) plus the payoff of playing against a Dove with probability \(1-p\),

\[ E(H,M)=p(V/2-C/2)+(1-p)V \tag{3.3}\]

Similarly the payoff of a Dove against M, \(E(D,M)\), is

\[ E(D,M)=0+(1-p)V/2 \tag{3.4}\]

All we have to do now is some algebra to replace Equation 3.3 and Equation 3.4 in Equation 3.2 and solve it for \(p\). One can do that by hand, or using Ryacas and solving Equation 3.3 with solve(),

library(Ryacas)
sol=solve(ysym("p*(V/2-C/2)+(1-p)*V==0+(1-p)*V/2 "),"p")
sol
{p==-(V-V/2)/(V/2-C/2-V-(-V)/2)}

This seems still a bit complicated, so let’s try to simplify the solution. We first have to remove the variable name to force simplify() to focus on the right hand side of sol,

simplify(y_rmvars(sol))
{V/C}

So the solution is \(p=V/C\). Therefore one should play Hawk more often when the resource value is larger, and less often when the cost of injury is higher. If the cost of injury is much larger than the value of the resource, one should just retreat and play Dove. Interestingly, if one considers a population of Hawks and Doves where no one is playing this mixed strategy, the population itself will evolve to a stable genetic polymorphism where a proportion \(p\) of the individuals play Hawk and a proportion \(1-p\) plays Dove.
